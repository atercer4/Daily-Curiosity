# Concept A v4 content and source register

Implementation date: October 20, 2026. Authority: [approved v4 brief, Decision 009](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1B9JH9ZwmSX2nxNa-hS2xWxB4DlzB7_a6v_8j68khWXE/edit), dated October 13, last modified October 14 at 04:04:36.805 UTC. Read from Drive before application changes.

## Editorial decisions

Exactly 20 cards, five per unchanged category. Five v3 topics remain; atomic seconds, musical octaves, and railway time were replaced to reduce the shared measurement/time/coordination motif. Venus and tree rings are the two remaining cards centered on time or dating. No category was renamed or added. Category identity comes from subject selection and writing, without new navigation, themes, or artwork.

Science asks about observed natural processes; engineering follows mechanisms and tradeoffs; arts attends to materials, performance, cultural participation, and spatial experience; history reads objects and institutions in their social context. The Gothic card centers on experienced light and imagery rather than repeating the bridge card’s force lesson. The banknote and stamp address different historical changes: representing value and prepaying delivery.

Hooks include mysteries, sensory observations, familiar experiences, historical reversals, comparisons, and material journeys. New Deep Dives begin with distinct questions or concrete explanations rather than a repeated Imagine formula. Of 20 Deep Dive openings, only the retained tree-ring card starts with Imagine; Rosetta uses a short hypothetical comparison later. Bridge and woodcut openings were lightly revised. Analogies remain where helpful and are not required for every card. All cards retain the same voluntary three beats, with roughly 20–35-word Hooks, 85–115-word Core Insights, and 130–175-word Deep Dives. Length is a guardrail, not evidence of editorial quality.

## Optional cover fields (Design System 1.0, issue #23)

Cards may add two optional fields for Today's cover (DESIGN_SYSTEM.md section 2):

- `question`: the italic cover headline. Wrap its key phrase in braces, for example `"Why would anyone launch a clock that’s {wrong on purpose?}"`; the braced phrase renders in cover gold. Use at most one braced phrase.
- `tease`: one or two sentences, more mysterious than explanatory.

Without them the cover shows the card's title and hook. Both are content and go through ChatGPT's fact-check (DESIGN_SYSTEM.md rule 3). No card has them yet; step 5 adds them.

### 21. Clocks set slow on purpose

- ID: `gps-relativity`; category: Engineering & Technology.
- Subject: Satellite navigation / relativity. Mechanism and tradeoff: two relativistic effects pull in opposite directions, and the net is corrected in the clock design before launch.
- Visual: Omitted: a diagram of two opposing rates would add little to three numbers stated in the text.
- [Ohio State University (Richard Pogge) — Real-World Relativity: The GPS Navigation System](https://www.astronomy.ohio-state.edu/pogge.1/Ast162/Unit5/gps.html): altitude about 20,000 km, speed about 14,000 km/h, −7 µs/day (speed), +45 µs/day (gravity), net +38 µs/day, clocks designed to tick slower, about 10 km/day error if uncorrected.
- [Physics Today (Neil Ashby, 2002) — Relativity and the Global Positioning System](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/relativity-and-the-global-positioning-system): "factory offset" before launch; more than 11 km of navigational error building up in one day, quickly making the system useless.
- Read on 11 November 2026. Also consulted, not linked: Ashby, "Relativistic Effects in the Global Positioning System" (AAPT-hosted PDF), which gives the same fractional rates.

### 22. Painting before the wall dries

- ID: `true-fresco`; category: Arts & Culture.
- Subject: Painting technique / materials. Practice behind the finished thing: the material's setting time shapes how the work is divided and how long it lasts.
- Visual: Omitted: a photograph of giornata seams would need an external image; the text describes them.
- [The National Gallery, London — Glossary: fresco](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/glossary/fresco): intonaco; paint applied to wet intonaco; only a day's plaster spread at once; giornata; a secco on dry plaster.
- [Encyclopaedia Britannica — Fresco painting](https://www.britannica.com/art/fresco-painting): pigments ground in water; colours set with the plaster and become a permanent part of the wall; buon fresco the most durable technique.
- [Wikipedia — Fresco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco): carbonation of lime (Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O) fixes the pigment; giornata seams usually visible, often from the ground; ten to twenty or more giornate in a wall-sized fresco; secco needs a binder (egg, glue, oil), is less durable, and some secco has fallen off.
- Source note: Britannica's direct read returned only the opening of the article, so the chemistry, seams and secco claims rest on the Wikipedia article. The fact-checker should confirm those claims or suggest a stronger museum or conservation source.

### 23. The smell waiting on the ground

- ID: `petrichor`; category: Science & Nature.
- Subject: Soil chemistry / fluid physics. Natural process: compounds that build up on dry ground are launched into the air by bubbles trapped in raindrops.
- Visual: Omitted: the champagne-bubble comparison in the text does the explaining; a diagram would need external slow-motion imagery to add anything.
- [Smithsonian Magazine — High-speed video shows when the smell of rain begins](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/high-speed-video-shows-when-smell-rain-begins-180953958/): Australian scientists first described petrichor in 1964; ichor and petros etymology; oils and other compounds from decaying matter attached to mineral and clay surfaces; geosmin produced by soil bacteria; high-speed cameras; drops trap tiny air bubbles that shoot upward and burst "in a fizz of aerosols", as in champagne; moderate or light rains on sandy or clay soils produce the most aerosols; published in Nature Communications.
- [EarthDate (Bureau of Economic Geology, UT Austin) — The scent of rain](https://www.earthdate.org/episodes/the-scent-of-rain): named petrichor in 1964 by Australian scientists; material attached to rock surfaces during dry spells; geosmin from soil bacteria (actinobacteria); humans detect a few parts per trillion; MIT 2015 finding; slower drops and more porous soils produce more aerosols; scent strongest after light rainfall on sandy soils.
- Read on 12 November 2026. Source note: MIT News (the original press release) and CSIRO pages could not be read by the agent's fetch tool in this run, so the names Bear and Thomas, the journal Nature, and experiment counts are deliberately left out of the card. The fact-checker may suggest the MIT News release (https://news.mit.edu/2015/rainfall-can-release-aerosols-0114) as a stronger primary source.

### 24. The receipts that burned Parliament

- ID: `exchequer-tallies`; category: History & Society.
- Subject: Public finance / institutions. Institutional arrangement: a medieval record-keeping system, abolished on paper, still had to be physically disposed of.
- Visual: Omitted: a photograph of a tally stick would need an external image; the text describes the split stick.
- [The History of Parliament — Burning the House down: the fire of 1834](https://historyofparliament.com/2013/10/16/the-fire-of-1834/): tally sticks as medieval Exchequer receipts, the Exchequer a government office based at Westminster; two large cartloads burned in the heating furnaces; chimney fire smouldered under the floor of the House of Lords chamber; a doorkeeper's wife spotted the flames; most of the medieval buildings destroyed; 16 October 1834; Prime Minister: "one of the greatest instances of stupidity upon record".
- [Wikipedia — Burning of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burning_of_Parliament): carved, notched wood, normally willow; split in two so both sides of an agreement had a record; 1782 Act requiring paper records, in force when the final sinecure-holder died in 1826; Treasury officials' instruction to clear the tallies; Weobley decided against giving them away as firewood; Joshua Cross and Patrick Furlong; the two heating furnaces directly below the peers' chamber; furnaces designed for coal, wood burns with a high flame; two tourists felt heat through their boots about 4 pm; flames spotted about 6 pm; Westminster Hall survived.
- Read on 12 November 2026. Source note: UK Parliament's Living Heritage pages and the full History of Parliament article could not be read by the agent's fetch tool in this run, so the Wikipedia article (which cites Caroline Shenton's history) carries the detailed claims. The fact-checker should confirm them or suggest the UK Parliament page as a stronger source.

## Source-read limitations and independent checks

Manual verification of all links and claim support remains part of independent QA. Automated direct reads returned anti-bot challenges for UNESCO’s two inscription pages and 403 responses for Smarthistory and the Postal Museum’s production Penny Black page. Their publisher-indexed text was available; UNESCO also provides the linked puppetry information sheet, and the postal card has a Smithsonian source. The Postal Museum’s indexed staging copy was consulted but is not linked in the app. British Museum source pages also require independent browser checking; earlier v3 readbacks had tool errors.

The DOE Heat Pump Systems page was indexed but its direct read returned 404; verify it manually before release. A second, directly readable DOE-hosted Massachusetts DOER handout is included for the transfer mechanism and heating/cooling operation. No blanket claim that every citation was live-link-tested is made. Publisher pages can change independently of this static prototype. No source content or remote services are requested until the reader follows a citation.
